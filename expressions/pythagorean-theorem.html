Pythagorean Theorem

a² + b² = c²

a² + b² = c² may be the most widely recognized theorem in school mathematics. Its simplicity hides both a rich geometric meaning and a complicated history that reaches beyond the person whose name it carries.

The story behind the Expression

What it says

For a right triangle with perpendicular side lengths a and b and hypotenuse c,

a² + b² = c²

The equation tells us that the square built on the hypotenuse has the same area as the two squares built on the legs combined. That geometric interpretation is one reason visual proofs of the theorem are so satisfying.

The history is older than the label

The theorem is traditionally associated with Pythagoras and the Pythagorean school, but knowledge of right-triangle numerical relationships predates them. Ancient Babylonian mathematics contains evidence of Pythagorean triples, and related geometric knowledge developed in multiple mathematical traditions. The exact historical role of Pythagoras is therefore less certain than the theorem’s familiar name suggests.

Why it keeps reappearing

The relation extends far beyond classroom triangles. It underlies the Euclidean distance formula, vector norms, coordinate geometry, trigonometric identities, and many measurement problems. In higher mathematics, related “Pythagorean” relationships appear whenever perpendicular components combine through sums of squares.

Why wear it?

Because almost everyone has seen the formula, but recognition comes in layers. One person remembers a worksheet. Another sees Euclidean geometry, vector length, distance in coordinate space, proofs, triples, and the structure of orthogonality. NerdWear likes Expressions that get better the more you know.
